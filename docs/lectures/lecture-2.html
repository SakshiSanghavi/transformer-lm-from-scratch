LECTURE 2 · REPRESENT WORDS AS VECTORS

Word embeddings:
meaning in geometry

Words are discrete symbols, but neural models work with numbers. Embeddings give each word a compact vector. By learning from context and co-occurrence, nearby vectors can capture useful semantic and syntactic patterns.

01 · From symbols to continuous features

A word ID has identity, not similarity

A one-hot vector of size |V| assigns one unique coordinate per vocabulary item. It represents identity cleanly, but the dot product between any two different one-hot vectors is zero: “car” is no more related to “truck” than to “tejuino.” A learned embedding maps each ID to a dense vector in ℝᵈ.

For a vocabulary with millions of entries and a few hundred dimensions, a dense embedding is far more compact than a one-hot feature per word. A lookup is differentiable with respect to the selected row, so training can adjust word vectors.

02 · Distributional semantics

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps”

The contexts around a word provide clues about its meaning. “Tejuino” in a bottle, something people like, something not to drink before driving, and something made from corn: recurring context patterns teach us about an unfamiliar word without a dictionary definition.

Context evidence
a bottle of ___ on the table
↓
people like ___
↓
made out of corn

Words sharing contexts get related representations.

Static vector limitation
bat → one vector
swings the bat
sees the bat

Same form, different sense. A single static vector must blur them together. Contextual embeddings depend on surrounding words.

Dense word vectors were often pretrained and then used to initialize NLP models. Modern language models still have an embedding lookup, but their later hidden states become context-sensitive. Subword tokenization also helps represent unseen word forms.
03 · Predict nearby context

Skip-Gram learns vectors by predicting neighbors

Given a center word, Skip-Gram tries to assign high probability to words appearing nearby. It learns a center-word vector and a context-word vector. If a word and context co-occur more than their independent frequencies predict, their association is informative.

center wordfox
→
score contextsquick, brown, jumps
→
learn vectorsword + context

One popular objective increases the score of observed word-context pairs and contrasts them with sampled negative pairs. The slide deck connects this to matrix factorization: the target matrix can be viewed as shifted pointwise mutual information (PMI), where negative sampling shifts PMI by log k.

Intuition: PMI asks “how much more often do w and c occur together than chance would predict?” Rare but tightly associated pairs can have high PMI.

The large word-context table has one row/column per vocabulary item. Word and context embeddings approximate its informative structure using far fewer dimensions.

04 · Fit global co-occurrence patterns

GloVe: learn from weighted log counts

GloVe uses a global word-word co-occurrence matrix. It learns a word vector and context vector so their dot product, plus biases, approximates the log of the count. A weighting function reduces the influence of extremely frequent pairs and caps the contribution of very large counts.

Skip-Gram

Predict a local context from a center word; negative sampling provides contrastive examples.

GloVe

Fit global co-occurrence counts with a weighted regression-style objective.

Both create useful spaces where semantic and syntactic relationships can emerge as directions, but their training signals and objectives differ.

05 · Evaluate the space

Similarity, analogies, tasks, and bias

Vector arithmetic intuition
king − man + woman ≈ queen
Paris − France + Japan ≈ Tokyo

If a relationship is represented consistently as a vector direction, adding/subtracting directions may recover an analogy. This is a diagnostic, not proof that a vector has a human-like definition.

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic
Intrinsic

Similarity ratings, analogy completion, vector geometry.

Extrinsic

Use embeddings inside a downstream model and evaluate task performance.

Bias comes from data and objectives. If social stereotypes occur in training text, distributional learning can encode them geometrically. For example, an axis derived from gender-associated terms may align with occupation vectors. “Learned from usage” does not mean “neutral” or “true.”

Evaluation should ask which properties a representation captures, whether it helps intended tasks, and which groups or contexts it represents poorly. Static embeddings also entangle word identity with a single average sense; contextual embeddings can vary with sentence meaning.

Revision check

Can you explain the embedding story?

Why is one-hot inconvenient for similarity?

Different one-hot vectors are orthogonal, so they do not express that “car” and “truck” are semantically related.

What is the Skip-Gram learning signal?

Use a center word to predict nearby context words, often contrasting true contexts with sampled negatives.

What does PMI compare?

Joint occurrence probability against the product of independent marginals; it measures association beyond chance.

What is the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic evaluation?

Intrinsic metrics probe vector properties directly. Extrinsic evaluation measures how useful embeddings are inside a downstream task model.

Why can “bat” be difficult for a static embedding?

It has different senses, but one type-level vector averages contexts. Contextual representations can encode the current sense.

Why should embedding bias be examined?

Vectors reflect patterns in their training corpora. Those patterns can reproduce harmful social associations or systematically underrepresent groups.
